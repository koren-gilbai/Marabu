A tutorial in political philosophy  ·  fourteen sessions

Reading Leo Strauss or, how to read between the lines without inventing what is there

Leo Strauss (1899–1973) taught a generation to read old books as if their authors had known exactly what they were doing. This lesson builds his thought from the ground up: from the opinions every community lives by, to the philosopher who questions them, to the careful art of writing that this tension produced, and from there to his diagnosis of modernity and its crisis.

Kirchhain 1899 · Annapolis 1973Berlin · Paris · Cambridge · New York · Chicago
The laws of our city were given by the gods to our first fathers, and what is ancient is good. A citizen who honors the gods and keeps the customs of his fathers lives justly and needs nothing further. Strangers bring strange teachings, but our law has never changed and never needs to. Let the young obey, let the old remember, and let no one in the marketplace ask what the fathers did not ask. For a city stands by its opinions about the just and the noble, and whoever loosens those opinions loosens the city itself.
Is the ancient good because it is old, or was it once honored because it was good? If our laws came from the gods, why do the laws of the Persians differ from ours, and who taught the fathers? Fire burns here and in Persia, but what men call just is not the same here and in Persia. What, then, is right by nature, and not merely by our agreement? The philosopher must ask what the citizen may not ask, and he must learn to ask it in such a way that the city still stands, and that the young who love to think can still find the question.

Prologue

How we will proceed

Strauss is usually taught from the top down, beginning with the slogans. We will build from the ground floor, the way he did.

Let me begin by telling you what I will not do. Strauss is often presented through a handful of famous and frightening phrases: esoteric writing, the noble lie, the crisis of the West, the quarrel of the ancients and the moderns. Begin with those and you will produce a caricature, either of a sage who possessed secrets or of a conspirator who taught others to keep them. Neither figure appears in the books.

On the voiceI speak to you here as a tutor in literature and philosophy would: with attention to how a text is made, not only to what it asserts. That is the right attitude for Strauss, who was above all a reader.

Instead we will start where Strauss himself insisted one must start, with what he called the natural, or pre-philosophic, understanding of political life: the opinions about the just and the unjust, the noble and the base, that every community lives by and that every child absorbs before learning to read. Nearly everything distinctive in Strauss grows from a single observation about those opinions. Philosophy is the attempt to replace them with knowledge, and no city can live on questions alone.

From that one tension, between the philosopher who must question and the city that must believe, we will derive the rest: why careful writers wrote carefully, why Strauss thought the modern project was a deliberate lowering of the human aim, why he regarded historicism and value-free social science as twin roads to nihilism, and why he believed that the vitality of Western civilization lay in a conflict, between Jerusalem and Athens, that must never be resolved.

You will find three kinds of material. Passages from Strauss are set off with a large opening quotation mark and a citation; read each twice, the second time more slowly. My explanations run in the main column, with pencil notes in the margin. The figures are working models. Move them, break them, and ask what Strauss's claim predicts. At the end of most sessions there is a question to check yourself, and at the end of the lesson a protocol for reading that you can carry into any old book.

One caution before we start. Strauss wrote almost nothing in the form of a treatise. He wrote commentaries: on Plato, Xenophon, Thucydides, Aristotle, Farabi, Maimonides, Machiavelli, Hobbes, Spinoza, Locke. His own views must be gathered from the way he reads others. To learn Strauss, then, is first of all to learn to read, and this lesson is organized accordingly.

Session I

The predicament: a young Jew in Weimar

Strauss's thought begins in a predicament. Should a human being live by reason or by revelation, and what happens to a political order that cannot decide?

Leo Strauss was born in 1899 in Kirchhain, a small town in Hesse, into an observant Jewish family. His education traces a map of German philosophy in the 1920s: the neo-Kantianism of Marburg, a doctorate under Ernst Cassirer in Hamburg, and then, in Freiburg in 1922, the lectures of Edmund Husserl and of a young assistant named Martin Heidegger, who was reading Aristotle in a way no one had heard before. For the rest of the decade Strauss worked as a researcher at the Academy for the Science of Judaism in Berlin, on Spinoza and on the collected works of Moses Mendelssohn.

The theologico-political problem

The phrase sounds forbidding, but the problem is simple to state. Two questions are knotted together. The first is theological: is the right way of life given to human beings by revelation, as divine law, or is it discovered by unassisted human reason? The second is political: who should rule, and by what authority? In the premodern world these were one question, because the laws of the community claimed divine origin. Modern liberalism tried to untie the knot. It made religion a private matter and built the state on reason and consent alone.

The knotTheology asks: by what authority should I live? Politics asks: by what authority should we be ruled? Wherever law claims to come from God, the two are the same question.

Strauss's generation watched that liberal solution fail in one particular place. The Weimar Republic granted Jews equal rights as individual citizens. But liberalism, by design, leaves society free, and German society was free to hate. A state that had renounced the authority to shape its citizens' souls proved too weak to protect a minority when the souls turned against it. Strauss would later describe his starting point with characteristic plainness.

Notice the word predicament. It is not a puzzle to be solved by a cleverer argument but a situation one is caught in. Strauss's first book asked whether Spinoza, the founder of the liberal solution, had actually refuted orthodox belief in revelation or had only made it look ridiculous. His answer, which he spent forty years refining, was that the Enlightenment had defeated orthodoxy in public opinion without refuting it in principle. Reason had claimed a victory it had not earned, and a reason that overclaims eventually discredits itself.

Read it twiceThe sentence draws a distinction that organizes his whole career: modern rationalism (Descartes, Hobbes, Spinoza) against premodern rationalism (Plato, Aristotle, Farabi, Maimonides). Strauss does not reject reason. He asks which reason.

Here is the program in one sentence. If modern reason destroys itself, perhaps the fault lies in how modern thinkers understood reason, and perhaps an older understanding, one more modest about what reason can prove and more aware of what it cannot, would hold. Strauss called the turn toward that older understanding a return. He did not mean nostalgia. He did not wish to restore the Greek city or the medieval community. He meant reopening a question that modernity believed it had closed.

The sentence about finite and infinite problems was written about the situation of the Jews. Liberal assimilation and political Zionism were each honorable answers, Strauss thought, but neither could abolish the deeper question of what it means to belong to a people constituted by a revealed law. The Jewish problem became, for him, the clearest case of the human problem: the question of how reason and revelation, and philosophy and the community, are to live together. Hold onto that. It returns in Session IX.

Why does Strauss call his problem "theologico-political" rather than simply "religious" or "political"?

Because the two questions cannot be separated. Whoever asks how a community should be governed must ask what authority stands behind its laws, and wherever the laws claim divine origin that is a theological question. Liberalism tried to separate them by privatizing religion; Strauss's experience of Weimar convinced him the separation was less stable than it looked.

Session II

The ground floor: opinion and the good

Every political act already contains a claim about the good. Political philosophy begins when someone takes that claim seriously enough to ask whether it is true.

Let us start lower than Strauss's biography, with something you did last week. Perhaps you signed a petition, argued about a tax, or simply complained about a law. Strauss asks us to notice that every such act carries a hidden cargo. Here is the opening of his essay "What Is Political Philosophy?", which I consider the best single entrance to his work.

The argument is a ladder, and each rung is forced by the one below it. Try it with an action of your own choosing.

Look closely at the fifth rung. The citizen does have a thought of the good, but it is an opinion (the Greek is δόξα, doxa): something held without being able to give a full account of why. Opinion is not mere error. Strauss is emphatic that opinions are the indispensable starting points of thought; they are fragments of the truth, held without their reasons. What defines philosophy is the attempt to move from those fragments to the whole.

Two words to keep apartPolitical thought is any reflection on political ideas, and it is indifferent to whether those ideas are opinion or knowledge. Political philosophy is the narrower thing: the deliberate effort to replace one with the other.

Now you can see why the Socratic question takes the form it does. Socrates asks the general, "What is courage?" He asks the priest, "What is piety?" He asks the young men at a festival, "What is justice?" Each person questioned already holds a confident opinion, and each opinion, examined, turns out to contain something true and something incoherent. The dialogue is the movement from the one to the other. For Strauss this is the original and still the natural form of political philosophy: it begins inside the city, in the city's own language, with questions that citizens already care about.

A Roman testimony

Cicero described the innovation of Socrates this way, in a sentence Strauss liked to recall: Socrates autem primus philosophiam devocavit e caelo et in urbibus conlocavit. In my translation: "Socrates was the first to call philosophy down from the heavens, to settle it in cities, and even to bring it into homes, and to compel it to inquire about life and morals and things good and bad" (Tusculan Disputations V.10). Before Socrates, philosophers studied the stars and the elements. Socrates turned to the opinions of the city, because he found there, in a confused but indispensable form, the clue to the whole.

This second formulation adds the decisive word: right. Political philosophy is not only descriptive. It asks which order is good, and it assumes that this question has an answer, or at least that it is a real question rather than a matter of taste. Much of the rest of Strauss's career is a defense of that assumption against the modern intellectual movements that denied it.

A politician declares, "I have no ideology; I am just being practical." What does Strauss's ladder show about this claim?

"Practical" means effective toward some end, and an end is something judged worth pursuing. The politician has not escaped the thought of the good; he has hidden it, perhaps even from himself. The ladder does not tell us his end is wrong. It tells us that he has one, and that it can be examined.

Session III

The discovery of nature

Before philosophy there is only "the way": the way of dogs, the way of a people. Philosophy begins by splitting that word in two.

To understand what Strauss means by natural right, the central term of his most famous book, we must go one floor lower still, to a world in which the idea of nature had not yet been discovered. Strauss asks us to imagine how human beings spoke before philosophy.

In that world, barking is the way of dogs in exactly the same sense in which a dietary law is the way of a people. Strauss's list is deliberately heterogeneous: it mixes what we would call nature, custom, and divine law, because before philosophy no one had yet pulled them apart. Both are simply what is done. And the way of one's own people is, without further question, the right way, because it is the way of the ancestors, and the ancestors were close to the gods. Strauss calls this the primeval identification of the good with the ancestral. It is not stupidity. It is the natural condition of every human community, including, in disguised forms, our own.

Physis, nomosφύσις (physis): nature, what grows of itself. νόμος (nomos): custom, convention, law. The whole of Greek political thought turns on this pair.

What breaks the spell? Strauss's answer is travel, and with it the discovery that other peoples have other ancestral ways, each claiming divine sanction. Herodotus tells a story that stages the moment exactly. The Persian king Darius summoned some Greeks, who burned their dead, and asked what price would persuade them to eat their fathers' corpses; they answered that nothing would. Then he summoned the Callatians, who ate their dead, and asked what would persuade them to burn their fathers; they cried out that he should not even speak of it. Herodotus concludes by quoting Pindar: custom is king of all (Histories III.38).

Now try to do what the first philosophers did. Sort the following "ways" by asking one question of each: is it the same everywhere?

Most items fall cleanly into one bin or the other. Two do not. Justice seems to vary from place to place like burial rites, yet everyone who uses the word means something that should not vary. And our ancestral law claims to come from the gods, which is to say from something higher than convention; but every people says this of its own law, and the laws contradict one another. The question "is justice by nature or only by convention?" is the founding question of political philosophy. Strauss calls the claim that something is right by nature, that there is a standard of right independent of what any city decrees, natural right.

Pause on the second sentence, because it explains why philosophy is politically dangerous from its birth. To ask what is right by nature, one must first doubt that the ancestral is simply right. But the authority of the ancestral is what holds most communities together. The philosopher's very first move is, from the city's point of view, an act of impiety.

A common objection holds that the variety of human opinions about justice proves there is no natural right. Strauss answers that the inference does not follow. Disagreement about the shape of the earth did not prove that the earth had no shape; it was the spur to finding out. The variety of codes is not a refutation of natural right. It is the experience that makes the question of natural right arise at all.

Why does Strauss insist that the idea of nature had to be discovered, rather than being obvious to everyone?

Because the pre-philosophic mind does not distinguish what is so by nature from what is so by custom; both are "the way." The distinction requires an experience (meeting peoples whose sacred ways contradict one's own) and an act of reflection (doubting the authority of the ancestral). Both are rare and both are risky, which is why philosophy appears late and in few places.

Session IV

The philosopher and the city

Philosophy dissolves opinion; the city rests on opinion. This is not a misunderstanding to be cleared up. It is a permanent tension, and Socrates died of it.

We now have two characters on the stage. The city (Strauss uses the Greek πόλις, polis, but he means any political community) lives by authoritative opinions about the just and the noble, and it must, because no community can make its laws depend on the outcome of an argument that may never end. The philosopher is the one who cannot stop asking whether those opinions are true. Strauss's claim is that the conflict between them is permanent: it belongs to the nature of things and not to some particular historical arrangement that a better constitution could repair.

The evidence is a trial. In 399 BC an Athenian jury condemned Socrates on the charge, as Plato's Apology reports it, of corrupting the young and of not believing in the gods the city believes in. Strauss read the charge with great seriousness. From the city's standpoint it was not absurd. A man who asks every citizen "What is piety?" and shows that no one can answer is, whatever his intentions, loosening the bonds of piety. Twenty-four years earlier the comic poet Aristophanes had already said as much in The Clouds, where Socrates hangs in a basket studying the heavens and teaches a young man to beat his father. Strauss devoted a whole book, Socrates and Aristophanes (1966), to the idea that the poet's mockery was a warning the philosopher took to heart.

Who learned?Strauss's suggestion is that Plato and Xenophon present a Socrates who has learned from Aristophanes: a Socrates concerned with the city, careful in speech, attentive to whom he is talking to. They present him, that is, as a political philosopher.

The two caves

Plato's image for the city is a cave (Republic, Book VII). Prisoners chained from birth watch shadows cast on a wall by objects carried before a fire, and they take the shadows for reality. The philosopher is the one who is turned around, climbs out into the sunlight, and must then decide whether to go back down. Strauss added a second floor beneath Plato's cave. Modern men, he suggested, are worse off than Plato's prisoners: below the natural cave of opinion, we have dug a second, artificial cave out of the accumulated doctrines of modern thought, above all the belief that all thought is the prisoner of its historical moment. Before we can even begin Plato's ascent, we must climb back up to the natural cave. The instrument of that first climb is the careful reading of old books.

Seventeen years later, in English, he returned to the image and sharpened it. The second cave is no longer an accident of history. It has been dug on purpose, by people afraid of the light.

Political philosophy in a double sense

If the tension between philosopher and city is permanent, the philosopher has a responsibility. He cannot stop thinking, but he can be careful about what he says, to whom, and how. This is why Strauss gives the phrase "political philosophy" a second meaning, and it is the one his readers most often miss.

Two senses1. Philosophy about politics: what is justice, what is the best regime. 2. Philosophy conducted politically: with prudence toward the city and as an invitation to the young. Strauss thinks the second sense is the more fundamental.

Read the dash in that sentence slowly. Political philosophy in the second sense is a manner: the way a philosopher speaks in public so as to protect the city from philosophy and philosophy from the city, while still opening a path for the few who are drawn to the questions. Plato's dialogues are the model. Strauss read the Republic less as a blueprint for a perfect state than as a demonstration of the limits of politics: the city that would be perfectly just requires so much that is against nature (philosopher-kings, the abolition of the family) that the dialogue cures the reader of the dream of realizing it. That cure is itself a political act, performed in writing. Which brings us to the most famous and most misunderstood of Strauss's ideas.

If the tension between philosophy and the city is permanent, why doesn't Strauss conclude that philosophers should simply withdraw from political life?

Because philosophy depends on the city (for leisure, for safety, for the young people it hopes to educate), and the city benefits from philosophy's moderating influence on its opinions. The answer is not withdrawal but a certain manner of speech: responsible, indirect, and pedagogical. That manner is what Strauss calls political philosophy in the second sense, and it is the subject of the next session.

Session V

Persecution and the art of writing

If the philosopher must think freely and the city must not be shaken, then a certain kind of book follows: one that says one thing to most readers and something more to a few.

We have arrived, by a long staircase, at the idea for which Strauss is best known. Note how we got here. We did not begin with a theory of secret meanings. We began with the opinions the city needs, the questions the philosopher cannot help asking, and the danger each poses to the other. Given those premises, careful writing is not a conspiracy. It is the natural form that responsibility takes when it has to be written down.

Strauss did not invent the idea. He rediscovered it in the 1930s, reading the medieval philosophers Farabi and Maimonides, who announced openly that they wrote with deliberate concealment. In the introduction to The Guide of the Perplexed (c. 1190), Maimonides lists seven causes of contradiction found in books and tells his reader that the contradictions in his own book are deliberate, due to the fifth and the seventh. He asks the reader to connect the chapters to one another and to weigh every word.

The question Strauss asked was simple: what if Maimonides was not unusual? What if, before the modern era of guaranteed free expression, this was how serious thinkers ordinarily wrote?

A German precedentStrauss had met the idea earlier in Lessing, whose dialogues Ernst and Falk (1778–80) speak of truths that the wise keep silent about. An essay Strauss wrote in 1939, "Exoteric Teaching," is about Lessing.

That passage is perfectly balanced, and the balance is the argument. A private letter is safe but reaches only friends. A public treatise reaches everyone, including the authorities. The art of writing combines the reach of the one with the safety of the other. How is this possible? Strauss's answer rests on an observation about readers that I would ask you to test against your own experience.

Four reasons to write this way

Persecution is the dramatic reason, but it is not the only one, and Strauss's later work makes clear that it is not the deepest. Arthur Melzer's study Philosophy Between the Lines (2014) sorts the motives into four, a typology that clarifies Strauss without distorting him.

  1. Defensive. To protect the writer from the authorities: the church, the prince, the party.
  2. Protective. To protect society from truths that might dissolve the opinions it lives by. Here the danger runs the other way, from philosophy to the city.
  3. Pedagogical. To protect the truth from being received passively. A conclusion you work out yourself is held differently from one you are handed; a puzzle in the text forces the reader to think.
  4. Political. Melzer's addition, characteristic of the Enlightenment: to advance a program of reform gradually, without announcing its full scope.

The second and third motives are the ones that matter most for Strauss. They do not depend on a censor. They would persist in the freest society, because they arise from the permanent difference between the philosopher and the city, and between the reader who is ready for a thought and one who is not.

A thought experiment from 1941

Strauss wrote the essay "Persecution and the Art of Writing" in New York in 1941, with the totalitarian states very much in view. To show that the technique is not an antiquarian curiosity, he imagined a contemporary case.

Two features of the thought experiment should be noticed. First, the author's statement of the forbidden view is, in Strauss's words, clearer and more compelling than any its own partisans had made, because he can drop "all the foolish excrescences" that grow on a doctrine once it has become comfortable. Esoteric writing is not vague. At its core it is more precise than the surface. Second, the readers are sorted not by a password but by attention. The censor reads conclusions. The young man who loves to think reads sentences.

The workbench: learning to notice

Here is a short treatise I wrote for you in imitation of the genre. No author wrote it, and nothing depends on its being historical; it is a training text, the way a musician practices on an étude. Read it once straight through, as you would read a newspaper. Then turn on the lenses one at a time.

If you tried all five lenses, you will have noticed that they converge. The contradiction shows that the laws were changed by human beings; the silence shows that a dangerous argument goes unanswered; the stranger asks the question the author never asks in his own name; the repetition quietly restricts the prohibition to "the many"; and the final sentence points to the center. No single signal proves anything. Their convergence is what gives an interpretation weight. That is the point Strauss makes when he insists that a reading between the lines must be more exact than a reading of the lines, not less.

The rules of evidence

Strauss's critics often accuse him of licensing any interpretation at all. Read what he actually wrote. Three sentences from the same essay function as rules of evidence, and they are strict.

The burden of proofNotice the qualifiers: a master of the art of writing; a blunder that would shame a high school boy; an author who discusses intentional blunders. The rule applies to Plato or Maimonides, not to a hurried pamphleteer.

The first rule is a principle of charity carried to its limit: the greater the writer, the less we may attribute an error to carelessness. The second is the literary scholar's first commandment: no sentence may be interpreted apart from its place in a whole. The third is a prohibition. Strauss's method begins from the surface and is bound by it, which is why he could write, in his book on Machiavelli, that the problem inherent in the surface of things, and only in the surface of things, is the heart of things.

The dialogue as the perfect esoteric form

Strauss's readings of Plato apply all of this to the dialogue form, and here a student of literature feels most at home. Plato never speaks in his own name. Every statement in a dialogue belongs to a character, spoken to a particular listener, at a particular moment, in a particular setting. The drama (who is present, what happens, who leaves) is as much a part of the meaning as the argument. Strauss titled his last book The Argument and the Action of Plato's Laws to make exactly this point.

Logographic necessity is the principle, stated by Socrates in the Phaedrus, that a well-made speech is like a living body, in which every part has its place and nothing could be moved without damage to the whole. Strauss turned the principle into a method. If a text is well made, then the place where something is said, the number of times it is said, and the character who says it are all part of what is said. Strauss even noticed that Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy contain 142 chapters, the number of books in Livy's history, and he did not think this an accident.

A friend says: "If authors hide their real views, then any interpretation is as good as any other." How would Strauss answer?

By pointing to his own rules of evidence. A legitimate reading between the lines starts from the explicit statements, attends to the whole work and its plan, applies only to masters of writing, and is forbidden wherever it would be less exact than a literal reading. Esotericism raises the standard of interpretation: one must account for every detail, including those a careless reading ignores. A good interpretation is one in which many independent signals converge.

Session VI

Ancients and moderns: the lowering of the aim

The moderns did not fall short of the ancient standard. They chose to lower it, so that it could be reached.

The quarrel between the ancients and the moderns was, in the seventeenth century, a literary dispute about whether modern poets could equal Homer. Strauss revived the phrase for a larger question: whether the founders of modern political philosophy had understood human beings better than Plato and Aristotle, or had merely decided to expect less of them. To see the quarrel, set the two positions side by side.

The classical position

For Plato and Aristotle, as Strauss reconstructs them in Natural Right and History, the purpose of political life is the perfection of human nature, which means virtue, and above all the virtue of the mind. Political philosophy is guided by the question of the best regime: the order in which the best human beings would rule. The classics knew perfectly well that such a regime is rarely if ever achieved. Its actualization depends on chance, on the rare coincidence of philosophy and power. So the classics paired a very high standard with great practical moderation. The best regime serves as a measure by which to judge actual regimes and to improve them modestly, not as a program to be imposed.

The modern revolution

Machiavelli, in Strauss's account, is the man who broke with this. Here is the famous passage from the fifteenth chapter of The Prince, in my translation from the Italian:

The "imagined republics" are Plato's and Aristotle's, and the "effectual truth" (la verità effettuale) is Machiavelli's slogan for a new beginning: take your bearings from how men actually live, not from how they ought to live. Strauss opened his book on Machiavelli with a deliberately unfashionable sentence.

Irony, againStrauss does not mean the sentence as a final verdict. He means that the "simple opinion" contains a truth the sophisticated readings forget, and his book then shows how much more there is in Machiavelli than that.

Hobbes completed the turn. Where the ancients built politics on the highest human possibility, Hobbes built it on the most powerful passion: the fear of violent death. In the state of nature, as he famously wrote, the life of man is "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (Leviathan, ch. 13). Everyone has a natural right to preserve himself, and from that right, not from any duty or any perfection, the sovereign state is constructed. Strauss's first English book, The Political Philosophy of Hobbes (1936), argued that this was a moral choice before it was a scientific one: Hobbes chose fear over pride as the foundation of politics. Notice the grammatical shift. Classical natural right (in the singular) is a standard of what is right. Modern natural rights (in the plural) are claims that individuals possess. The center of gravity moves from duty to right.

Locke, finally, softened Hobbes. Mere self-preservation became comfortable self-preservation, and the engine of society became the acquisition of property. Strauss's summary of the Lockean condition is one of his most memorable phrases.

Strauss's most compact statement of the move comes from "What Is Political Philosophy?":

Here is the structure of the modern move in a single figure. Slide from the ancients toward Locke and watch two things happen at once: the aim comes down, and the likelihood of reaching it goes up.

It would be a serious misreading to conclude that Strauss despised the modern achievement. The modern bargain worked, and spectacularly: security, prosperity, toleration, and the scientific conquest of nature are its fruits. The American founding is, in large part, its child. When Madison writes in Federalist 51 that "if men were angels, no government would be necessary," and that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition," he is designing institutions that work without requiring virtue, which is precisely the modern project at its most intelligent. Strauss's question was not whether the bargain worked but what it cost: whether a civilization that has lowered its aim can still say why its achievements are good.

Nor did Strauss imagine that the ancients could simply be applied to modern problems. He turned to them, he said, because of a modern crisis, and he warned against expecting too much from them.

What is the difference between "natural right" and "natural rights," and why does Strauss care about the shift?

"Natural right" in the classical sense is a standard: what is right by nature, which may impose duties and points toward human perfection. "Natural rights" in the modern sense are individual claims, grounded in self-preservation, that come before any duty. The shift moves the foundation of politics from the highest human possibility to the strongest human need. It makes politics more reliable and less elevated, and it leaves open the question of what the rights are for.

Session VII

The three waves of modernity

Each wave of modern thought begins as a critique of the one before it, and each carries the modern project further from nature.

In a late lecture, "The Three Waves of Modernity" (published in 1975), Strauss compressed his account of modern thought into a single image. Modernity did not unfold as a smooth progress. It came in three waves, each a radical critique of its predecessor, and each, paradoxically, a further step in the same direction. The lecture opens with a diagnosis.

Read the waves as an argument in three moves.

The first wave we have already met. "One must start from how men do live; one must lower one's sights," is how Strauss summarizes Machiavelli's counsel; under the new dispensation, he adds, "the political problem becomes a technical problem." Machiavelli lowers the standard; Hobbes and Locke build the modern state on self-preservation and acquisition. Nature is still the reference point, but it is now something to be mastered rather than a model to be imitated.

The second wave begins with Rousseau, who attacks the first wave in the name of virtue, of Sparta and republican Rome against the bourgeois. But Rousseau cannot return to classical nature, because he has concluded that human nature is almost infinitely malleable: what we are, we have become through history. The standard is therefore no longer nature but the general will, and later, in Kant and Hegel, reason as it realizes itself in history. Its political expression, in Marx, is the hope that history will end in a rational and free society.

The third wave begins with Nietzsche, who accepts that all standards are historical creations and draws the conclusion that history is not rational at all. There is no end of history and no reason in it; there are only values, created by acts of will. The sentiment of existence is no longer, as in Rousseau, harmony and peace, but terror and anguish. Heidegger radicalizes this. Its political expression, Strauss says without euphemism, was fascism.

The lecture does not end in despair. Its final turn is the one Strauss's readers most often forget. Liberal democracy, he says, has a resource that communism and fascism lack: it draws support from a way of thinking that is not modern at all, the premodern tradition of the West. That is the political reason for the return to the ancients. It is not a project to replace liberal democracy but an attempt to strengthen it from a root it no longer recognizes as its own.

Why does Strauss describe each wave as both a reaction against the previous one and a radicalization of it?

Rousseau reacts against Hobbes and Locke in the name of virtue, but he does so by making human nature a product of history, which moves even further from nature as a standard. Nietzsche reacts against Rousseau and Hegel by denying that history is rational, but he does so by making all standards acts of will, which completes the abandonment of nature. Each critique is sincere, and each takes the modern premise one step further.

Session VIII

Two dogmas, and where they lead

Modern social thought rests on two doctrines: that all thought is historical, and that facts can be known while values cannot. Strauss argues that each undermines itself, and that together they end in nihilism.

Natural Right and History opens, unexpectedly, with the Declaration of Independence. "We hold these truths to be self-evident," Strauss quotes, and then asks whether the nation that was founded on that sentence still believes it. His worry was that American social science had quietly imported, from defeated Germany, the two doctrines that made such a sentence unsayable.

The German yokeThe thought he means is the historicism of the nineteenth-century German historical school and the value-free social science of Max Weber, both then at the height of their prestige in American universities.

First dogma: historicism

Historicism is not the study of history. Strauss was a historian of ideas all his life. Historicism is the doctrine that all thought is the product of its historical situation, so that no thought can claim to be simply true: Plato's ideas are Greek, Aquinas's natural law is medieval, Locke's natural rights are bourgeois. The doctrine seems humble and sophisticated. Strauss's first objection is logical, and you can test it with the figure below.

Strauss knew that the logical refutation does not dispose of the serious form of historicism. Heidegger's radical historicism accepts that its own insight is historical. It claims that the insight into the historicity of all thought was granted at a unique moment, a moment in which the character of all previous thought has become visible. Strauss's reply is that such a moment would have to be, in effect, an end of history: a privileged standpoint from which the whole is seen. Radical historicism must therefore claim for itself exactly the kind of trans-historical knowledge it denies to Plato.

Second dogma: the fact-value distinction

The second doctrine comes from Max Weber, whom Strauss treated as the most formidable modern social scientist and therefore the one most worth refuting. Weber held that science can establish facts and the efficient means to given ends, but it cannot establish which ends are good. Values are chosen, not known. Social science must be value-free. Strauss states his thesis with unusual bluntness.

His second argument is more literary, and in my view more devastating. A value-free description of human things is not neutral. It cannot even be written without becoming absurd. Try it.

The phrase "bitter satire" is the work of a man who read literature. A scrupulously neutral account of evil reads like Swift. And the failure is not only stylistic. A science that cannot name tyranny cannot see it. Strauss wrote On Tyranny, a commentary on a short dialogue by Xenophon, in the immediate aftermath of the Second World War, and its introduction contains the charge in its starkest form.

A decade later he summarized his view of the behavioral political science of the 1950s in a passage whose wit should not hide its gravity.

Where the two dogmas lead

Put the two doctrines together. If all thought is historical, no standard is simply true. If values are chosen rather than known, no standard can be defended by reason. What remains is preference. Strauss's word for this condition is nihilism, and the introduction to Natural Right and History describes its psychology with great precision.

The last sentenceThis is the counterintuitive claim. One expects relativism to make people tolerant and mild. Strauss predicts the opposite: people who can no longer give reasons for their commitments will hold them fanatically, because reason has become the enemy of commitment.

He had seen it happen. In a lecture delivered at the New School in February 1941, "German Nihilism," Strauss tried to explain to an American audience why so many intelligent young Germans had welcomed National Socialism. He did not describe them as nihilists in the sense of wanting to destroy everything. Their nihilism was specific: it was a moral protest against modern civilization itself.

Read that sentence with care, because it is a warning in two directions. It explains the appeal of a catastrophe without excusing it, and it implies that a liberal civilization which offers its young nothing higher than production and consumption invites the same revolt. This is why Strauss, the critic of liberal relativism, could also call himself its friend.

Isn't relativism the basis of tolerance? Why does Strauss call liberal relativism "a seminary of intolerance"?

Because tolerance is itself a value, and relativism cannot say why it is better than intolerance. If all values are equally groundless, then the choice of intolerance is as legitimate as the choice of tolerance. Tolerance was originally defended on natural right grounds (a claim about what human beings are owed). Cut off from those grounds, it has nothing to say to someone who rejects it, and it trains its students to believe that nothing can be said.

Session IX

Jerusalem and Athens

The West has two roots that cannot be reconciled: the life of obedient love and the life of free insight. Its vitality depends on the tension staying alive.

We return now to the predicament with which we began in Session I, but from a height. Strauss's mature formulation of the theologico-political problem is a confrontation between two cities, which stand for two ways of life.

Both cities answer the same question: what is the one thing needful, the thing without which a human life is not worth living? Their answers agree about a great deal of morality. Both condemn murder, theft, and adultery; both praise justice. They disagree about the root.

Now the decisive and difficult step. Can either side refute the other? Strauss's answer is that neither has. Revelation cannot refute philosophy, because philosophy does not accept any premise that it cannot examine. But philosophy cannot refute revelation either, because revelation claims to be a free act of an omnipotent God, which by its nature cannot be ruled out by reason. In Natural Right and History he describes how the situation looks from philosophy's side; the passage is part of his reconstruction of what led Weber to despair, and Strauss frames it with "would," but it is the hardest challenge his own position faces.

A live disputeWhether Strauss believed philosophy could finally meet this challenge is among the central quarrels of his interpreters. Heinrich Meier argues that the whole of Strauss's work is a defense of the philosophic life against it; others read him as holding the question permanently open.

If neither side can refute the other, then a synthesis is the worst of the available options. Every attempt to combine the two (to make faith rational or reason pious) ends by subordinating one to the other while pretending to honor both. Strauss's conclusion is that each of us must choose, and remain open to the other.

The image I use for this is an old gardener's technique. To draw an ellipse, fix two pins in the ground, tie a loop of string around them, and pull a pencil taut against the string as you walk. The curve exists only because there are two centers and because the string is under tension. The figure is mine, not Strauss's, but it states his claim exactly.

A biographical note belongs here, because Strauss's position is easy to misread in both directions. Strauss was not an observant Jew in his adult life, and he wrote as a philosopher. But he never treated orthodoxy with the condescension of the Enlightenment, and he regarded the claim of revelation as a permanent challenge that philosophy must meet rather than mock. In that respect his "return" was a return to the question, not to either answer.

Why does Strauss reject a "synthesis" of reason and revelation, even though both agree on much of morality?

Because they disagree about the root: what makes a life good and how the highest is known. Any synthesis has to decide, in the end, which authority is final, and so it subordinates one to the other while claiming to honor both. Strauss thinks the honest course is to choose one way of life while remaining genuinely open to the challenge of the other. The tension, kept alive, is what keeps Western civilization from settling into either dogmatism.

Session X

Problems, not solutions

For Strauss, philosophy is not a doctrine. It is awareness of the permanent problems, and it ends the moment a solution seems more certain than the problem it answers.

If you have followed the lesson so far, you may have formed a picture of Strauss as a man with a system: natural right against historicism, ancients against moderns, Jerusalem against Athens. That picture is half right. The other half is contained in what I regard as the most important paragraph he wrote about philosophy itself.

The argument compares two quantities: how evident the problem is, and how evident any solution is. Move the slider to strengthen your attachment to a solution.

What are the "fundamental and comprehensive problems"? Strauss names a small number again and again: the nature of the whole; the relation between the whole and its parts; the question of the best way of life; the tension between philosophy and the city; reason and revelation. They are permanent in the sense that every age meets them in a new guise and none has dissolved them. This, and not a doctrine, is what Strauss meant by the "perennial" in philosophy. It is also why he could say that what the history of philosophy teaches is not the relativity of all answers but the permanence of the questions.

The quarrel with Kojève

The paragraph you just read comes from a debate, conducted in print between 1948 and 1954, with Strauss's friend Alexandre Kojève, the Russian-French philosopher whose lectures on Hegel had shaped a generation of Parisian intellectuals. Their occasion was Xenophon's Hiero, a dialogue in which the poet Simonides advises the tyrant of Syracuse on how to rule well. Kojève argued, as a Hegelian, that history moves toward a "universal and homogeneous state," in which all human beings are recognized as free and equal, and that the philosopher must act in history to bring it about. Strauss answered that such a state would be the most complete tyranny ever known, precisely because there would be no outside to it.

An afterlifeThe idea of the "end of history" reached American readers through this circle. Francis Fukuyama, a student of Strauss's student Allan Bloom, built his famous 1989 essay on Kojève's reading of Hegel.

The disagreement is the zetetic principle applied to politics. Kojève believed the problem of the best regime could be solved, by history. Strauss believed it could not, and that a political order claiming to have solved it would leave no place to stand for anyone who still asked the question. The philosopher's freedom depends on the existence of more than one city.

Strauss is often described as a dogmatist defending "absolute values." How does the Restatement paragraph complicate that description?

It defines the philosopher as someone for whom the problems are always more evident than the solutions, and it calls the person who becomes certain of a solution a sectarian. Strauss defended the reality of the questions about the good against those who said they were meaningless; he did not claim to possess the answers. The defense of natural right is, at its core, the defense of the question.

Session XI

Liberal education: listening to a quarrel

Liberal education means listening to the conversation of the greatest minds. Because they contradict one another, the listener is forced to become a judge.

Strauss's most public teaching was not about esotericism or the ancients. It was about education, and it was addressed to adults: in 1959 he gave a commencement address to the graduates of the University of Chicago's Basic Program of Liberal Education for Adults. It is the gentlest of his texts and, for students of literature, the most immediately useful.

So far this sounds like a defense of the canon, and it is. But the next step turns the canon from a monument into a problem. The greatest minds do not agree. On every question that matters most (the nature of the good, the origin of political order, the place of the gods) they contradict one another.

That is the predicament of every serious student, stated exactly. The great authors speak, as Strauss puts it, in monologues; each wrote his book alone. It is the reader who turns their monologues into a dialogue, by setting one beside another and asking which is right. The reader is not competent to judge, and must judge anyway. The only way through is to read each author so carefully that one understands him as he understood himself, which is the discipline of the previous sessions. Try listening to a few of the quarrels.

Strauss's claims for liberal education are high, and he stated them in a democratic register. Liberal education is not training for an elite that already exists. It is an attempt to form one, open to anyone willing to read, within a society that might otherwise forget what human excellence looks like.

Weber, againNotice that Strauss borrows his description of mass culture from Weber, the thinker he criticized most severely. He did not hesitate to learn from opponents. That too is a lesson in reading.

The word "aristocracy" is provocative, and Strauss meant it to be. But read the second sentence: the aristocracy is defined by having ears to hear, not by birth or wealth. And in a companion essay he made his attitude toward democracy as clear as he ever made anything.

If the great books contradict each other, why read them at all rather than simply choosing the most persuasive modern account?

Because their disagreements are about the fundamental alternatives, stated by the minds best able to state them. A modern textbook usually presents one answer as settled; the great books show you the question with its strongest rival answers. Reading them does not hand you a conclusion. It makes you competent, slowly, to reach one, and it frees you from the unexamined opinions of your own time, which is what Strauss meant by liberation.

Session XII

Strauss among the émigrés

Strauss belongs to a remarkable generation of German thinkers formed in Weimar and scattered by 1933. Reading him beside them shows what was distinctive in his choice.

For a student of German intellectual history, the most illuminating way to place Strauss is to set him in his cohort. Many of these figures sat in the same lecture halls in Marburg and Freiburg in the 1920s, read the same books, and faced the same crisis: the collapse of the liberal order and the apparent bankruptcy of the reason that was supposed to sustain it. They gave strikingly different answers.

Schmitt: the critique that did not go far enough

Strauss's "Notes" on Schmitt's The Concept of the Political (1932) is a young man's essay of extraordinary penetration. Schmitt had defined the political by the distinction between friend and enemy and had attacked liberalism for trying to neutralize it. Strauss agreed that liberalism evades the political, but argued that Schmitt's own position (an affirmation of the political as such, of seriousness as such, regardless of what one is serious about) was itself a kind of liberal neutrality turned inside out. Heinrich Meier has shown that Schmitt quietly revised his book in response.

Read the middle sentence again: in such a horizon Hobbes completed the foundation of liberalism. The way out of liberalism, for the young Strauss, leads back through its founder, and through Hobbes to the premodern world Hobbes rejected. That is the itinerary of the next four decades in three sentences.

Heidegger: the only great thinker

Strauss never pretended that Heidegger was a minor figure, and he never forgave him his politics. In a lecture of 1956 he recalled his first encounter with Heidegger in Freiburg, and his remark to Franz Rosenzweig at the time.

"Great trouble"The trouble is that the greatest thinker of the age had embraced National Socialism in 1933. For Strauss this was not a biographical accident but a question about Heidegger's philosophy, which had no place for natural right or for moderation.

Adorno: the same diagnosis, the opposite direction

Here I permit myself a comparison that Strauss did not make. In 1944 Horkheimer and Adorno, émigrés in California, finished Dialectic of Enlightenment, whose thesis is that the reason of the Enlightenment, in the very act of liberating human beings from myth, became a new mythology of domination. Twenty years later Strauss wrote of the "self-destruction of reason" as the outcome of modern rationalism. Both diagnoses describe reason turning against itself; both include a harsh judgment on mass culture. Yet the remedies point in opposite directions. Critical theory sought to redeem the Enlightenment's promise through a more self-critical reason oriented toward a future emancipation, and it would have regarded Strauss's appeal to nature as ideology. Strauss would have regarded critical theory's reliance on a philosophy of history as a late form of the second wave. Reading the two side by side is one of the best exercises I know in understanding what each thought was at stake.

Session XIII

Quarrels about Strauss

Strauss is among the most argued-about political philosophers of the last century. Some quarrels are serious; some concern a phantom. A careful reader should be able to tell which.

The letter of 1933

The most troubling document is a private letter Strauss wrote from Paris to Karl Löwith on 19 May 1933, months after Hitler came to power. It was first published in 2001, in the third volume of Strauss's collected writings, edited by Heinrich Meier. In the widely quoted translation by Scott Horton, the key passage reads:

The letter must be read, and read without apology. It shows a young émigré who despised the liberal appeal to the rights of man, at the very moment he was being driven out of his country by the regime he called a "shabby abomination." Interpreters divide over its weight. Some, such as William Altman, see a continuity that runs through the mature work. Others, including Steven B. Smith and Catherine and Michael Zuckert, read the later writings (with their defense of liberal democracy as the best available regime and their praise of the American founding) as the outcome of a real development. The honest course is to hold the letter in view while reading the books, and to let the books answer for themselves.

Does esoteric writing exist?

In 1985 the classicist Myles Burnyeat published a scathing review of Strauss in The New York Review of Books under the title "Sphinx Without a Secret." His complaint was that esoteric interpretation cannot be falsified: any evidence against a hidden teaching can be read as part of the concealment. The objection is serious, and Strauss's own rules of evidence (Session V) are his best answer to it. The historical half of the question has since shifted. Arthur Melzer's Philosophy Between the Lines (2014) assembled a large body of testimony, from antiquity to the eighteenth century, in which philosophers openly describe writing esoterically or reading others who did. It is now hard to deny that the practice existed. Whether a particular text conceals a particular teaching remains, as Strauss said, a matter for exact argument in each case.

Elitism and the noble lie

Critics such as Shadia Drury (The Political Ideas of Leo Strauss, 1988) read Strauss as teaching that a wise elite should govern through salutary falsehoods. The charge confuses, in my view, a description of how classical authors understood the relation between philosophy and the city with a program for modern government. But it is fair to say that Strauss's reticence about his own views invites suspicion, and that some admirers have treated his writings as a doctrine for initiates. Strauss's public teaching, it should be remembered, was a defense of liberal education and of liberal democracy against its flatterers.

The neoconservative phantom

In 2003, during the debate over the Iraq war, a number of journalists described Strauss as the hidden philosopher of American foreign policy, on the ground that some officials had studied with him or with his students. Strauss had died thirty years earlier and wrote almost nothing about contemporary foreign policy. Scholars of every political orientation who have studied his work, from Mark Lilla to Steven B. Smith, have rejected the picture of a secret doctrine passed down to policymakers, while acknowledging the real influence of his students on American conservative thought. The episode is best studied as an instance of what Strauss himself warned against: careless reading.

The schools

Strauss's students and their students have divided along lines that are often labeled geographically. So-called West Coast Straussians, around Harry Jaffa and the Claremont Institute, read the American founding as grounded in natural right and Lincoln as its greatest statesman. East Coast Straussians, around Allan Bloom, Harvey Mansfield, and Thomas Pangle, emphasize the modern, Lockean character of the founding and the permanent tension between philosophy and politics. A third group, sometimes called Midwestern, has devoted itself chiefly to the philosophic interpretation of texts, with Seth Benardete as its most original reader. The labels are rough, but they show that Strauss left behind not a doctrine but a set of questions on which his heirs disagree.

Session XIV

Your turn: a reader's protocol

Strauss's most transferable gift is a way of reading. Here it is as a procedure, in the order in which the steps should be taken.

  1. Begin with the surface. Read the explicit statements as if the author meant them, because usually he did. Summarize the argument in his own terms before you look for anything else.
  2. Establish the genre and the speakers. Treatise, dialogue, commentary, letter, speech? In a dialogue, who speaks, to whom, when, and in whose presence? Attribute nothing to the author that belongs to a character.
  3. Map the plan. Outline the whole. Count the chapters, find the center, compare the beginning with the end. Note where the author places his most and least emphatic statements.
  4. Collect the anomalies. Contradictions, silences, odd repetitions, apparent blunders, examples that undercut their own point. Collect them first; do not interpret yet.
  5. Look for convergence. One anomaly is a curiosity. Several independent anomalies pointing in the same direction are evidence. Interpret only where they converge, and only so far as they converge.
  6. Understand the author as he understood himself. Before you ask whether he was right, make sure you could state his position in a form he would accept. This is the discipline Strauss defended against Gadamer.
  7. Then judge. Ask whether he was right, and set him in conversation with his strongest opponent. Remember the prohibition: a reading between the lines that is less exact than a reading of the lines is forbidden.

A last word from me as your tutor. You will be tempted, after a lesson like this, to read every text as a coded message. Resist it. Strauss's method is not suspicion. It is attention: the kind of attention that assumes a great author knew what he was doing, and that holds the reader responsible for noticing. Most of what you find will be on the surface, once you have learned to see the surface. That is what Strauss meant when he wrote that the problem inherent in the surface of things, and only in the surface of things, is the heart of things.
